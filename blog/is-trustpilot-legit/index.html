Is Trustpilot Legit? An Honest Assessment for Business Owners

Is Trustpilot legit? Yes, but with important caveats about how verification works, fake review risks, pricing transparency, and what "verified" actually means on the platform.

2026-08-07

Is Trustpilot legit? It's one of the most searched questions about the platform — and for good reason. Trustpilot is a publicly traded company (LSE: TRST) with over 300 million reviews and 1.4 million business domains listed. It's a legitimate, regulated business.

But "legit" has layers. The real question most business owners are asking is: can I trust the reviews on Trustpilot, and is it worth the money for my business? The answers to those questions are more nuanced than a yes or no.

# Is Trustpilot a legitimate company?

Yes. Trustpilot was founded in 2007 in Copenhagen, Denmark. It went public on the London Stock Exchange in 2021. It employs over 900 people, operates in 10+ countries, and is regulated under UK and EU law as a publicly listed company. It's not a scam or a fly-by-night operation.

Trustpilot also publishes a transparency report, maintains a content integrity team, and has invested in automated fake review detection. In 2024, Trustpilot reported removing 3.3 million fake reviews — roughly 6% of all reviews submitted to the platform.

So: Trustpilot the company is legit. Now let's talk about the reviews on it.

# Can I Trust Trustpilot Reviews? What Business Owners Need to Know

If you're asking 'can I trust Trustpilot reviews,' the short answer is sometimes — it depends on whether reviews are verified and how the business collects them. Below we break down exactly when Trustpilot reviews are trustworthy and when they are not.

This is where it gets complicated. Trustpilot reviews come in two types:

# Organic reviews (unverified)

Anyone with a Trustpilot account can leave an organic review for any business on the platform. No proof of purchase, no customer relationship verification — just a name, a rating, and text. Trustpilot does not verify that the reviewer is actually a customer.

This is the source of most Trustpilot controversy. Businesses can receive:

  • Fake positive reviews from the business itself or agencies it hires
  • Fake negative reviews from competitors or disgruntled non-customers
  • Review bombing — coordinated campaigns to flood a business with 1-star reviews
  • Extortion reviews — negative reviews posted to pressure the business into paying for services or refunds

Trustpilot's automated detection catches some of these, but it's a reactive system. The review goes live first and may be removed later. During that window — which can be days or weeks — the fake review is visible to every potential customer.

# Invited reviews (email-verified)

Businesses on paid Trustpilot plans can send review invitations to their customer lists. These reviews are marked as "Verified" — meaning Trustpilot confirmed the reviewer clicked a link sent to a specific email address.

This is stronger than organic reviews but still has limitations:

  • The business controls who gets invitations — they can cherry-pick happy customers
  • "Verified" means email confirmed, not purchase confirmed
  • The reviewer could be anyone with access to that inbox, not necessarily the customer
  • There's no independent verification that a transaction actually occurred

# The "Trustpilot reviews are fake" problem

Search "Trustpilot reviews are fake" and you'll find thousands of articles, forum threads, and social media posts. The perception problem is real. The issue isn't that Trustpilot itself is fake — it's that the platform's openness makes it vulnerable to manipulation, and consumers know it.

A 2025 survey by Which? (UK consumer advocacy group) found that only 48% of UK consumers trust reviews on Trustpilot — lower than Google Reviews (56%) and significantly lower than verified-purchase reviews on Amazon (72%).

# The hidden costs

  • No free trial on paid plans
  • Annual contracts with auto-renewal — cancellation requires notice
  • Add-on costs for features like Google Shopping integration, additional locations, and API access
  • No refund detection — if a customer gets a refund, you must manually flag and remove their review
  • Time investment — managing your Trustpilot presence (responding to reviews, flagging fakes, monitoring) takes staff hours

# What Trustpilot does well

To be fair, Trustpilot has real strengths:

  1. Brand recognition: Trustpilot is the most recognized review platform globally after Google and Amazon. Displaying a Trustpilot star rating carries brand weight.
  2. Google integration: Trustpilot star ratings appear in Google Ads, Google Shopping, and organic search results — this drives click-through rates.
  3. Transparency reports: Trustpilot publishes more transparency data than most review platforms, including fake review removal statistics.
  4. Consumer awareness: Trustpilot's brand means consumers know they can leave reviews — which can drive volume if your customers are Trustpilot users.
  5. Content integrity team: Unlike many platforms, Trustpilot employs real people to investigate review disputes.

These are legitimate advantages. For some businesses — particularly large brands in consumer-facing industries — Trustpilot's brand recognition and Google integration justify the cost.

# What to watch out for

  1. Reviews can appear without your knowledge: Anyone can create a Trustpilot profile for your business and start collecting reviews — including negative ones. You don't control whether your business appears on Trustpilot.
  2. Fake reviews are persistent: Despite Trustpilot's detection systems, fake reviews slip through. Removing them requires flagging and waiting for Trustpilot's content integrity team to investigate.
  3. The "pay to play" dynamic: Businesses on paid plans get tools to manage reviews (invitations, analytics, Google integration). Businesses on free plans get organic reviews — including negative ones — with limited recourse. This creates an incentive to pay up.
  4. Pricing opacity: Trustpilot doesn't publish transparent pricing. You need a sales call to get a quote, and pricing varies significantly by business size and industry.
  5. Verification is email-only: Even on paid plans, "verified" means email-verified, not purchase-verified. There's no way for a consumer to know if the reviewer actually bought the product.

# Should your business use Trustpilot?

# Use Trustpilot if:

  • You're a large consumer brand and the Google integration alone justifies the cost
  • Trustpilot brand recognition matters more to your customers than verification strength
  • You have the staff bandwidth to actively manage your Trustpilot presence (responding to reviews, flagging fakes)
  • You're in an industry where Trustpilot is the dominant review platform (primarily UK/European consumer goods and services)

# Consider alternatives if:

  • You want review verification backed by actual payment data, not just email
  • You're cost-sensitive — Trustpilot's paid plans start at ~$299/month
  • You want automatic refund detection so refunded customers' reviews don't stay up
  • You want cryptographic proof that reviews haven't been tampered with
  • You want to control when review invitations send based on actual purchase events (through Stripe)

# FAQ: is Trustpilot legit?

# Is Trustpilot a scam?

No. Trustpilot is a legitimate, publicly traded company (LSE: TRST) regulated under UK and EU law. It's not a scam. But the reviews on it can be — and that's the distinction that matters.

# Can businesses delete bad reviews on Trustpilot?

No. Businesses cannot delete reviews. They can flag reviews that violate Trustpilot's guidelines, and Trustpilot's content integrity team decides whether to remove them. However, businesses on paid plans can influence which reviews are most visible by inviting more customers to leave reviews — effectively pushing negative reviews down.

# Does Trustpilot verify that reviewers are real customers?

No. Trustpilot verifies email addresses, not purchases. A "Verified" review on Trustpilot means the reviewer clicked a link sent to a specific email address. It does not mean Trustpilot confirmed a purchase occurred. Anyone with access to that inbox — including non-customers — can leave a verified review.

# Why does Trustpilot have so many fake reviews?

Trustpilot's open model — anyone can review any business — makes it vulnerable to fake reviews by design. It's the tradeoff for having a large volume of reviews across many businesses. Trustpilot invests in automated and human review detection, but the openness of the platform means fake reviews will always be a cat-and-mouse game.

# Is Trustpilot worth the price?

It depends on your business. For large consumer brands where Trustpilot's Google integration drives measurable ROI, the cost can be justified. For small to medium businesses, the combination of email-only verification, high price point, and management overhead means alternatives often deliver better value.
